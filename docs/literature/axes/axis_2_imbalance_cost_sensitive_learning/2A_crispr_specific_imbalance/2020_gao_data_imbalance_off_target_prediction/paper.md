## **Abstract**

For genome-wide CRISPR off-target cleavage sites (OTS) prediction, an important issue is data imbalance—the number of true OTS recognized by whole-genome off-target detection techniques is much smaller than that of all possible nucleotide mismatch loci, making the training of machine learning model very challenging. Therefore, computational models proposed for OTS prediction and scoring should be carefully designed and properly evaluated in order to avoid bias. In our study, two tools are taken as examples to further emphasize the data imbalance issue in CRISPR off-target prediction to achieve better sensitivity and specificity for optimized CRISPR gene editing. We would like to indicate that (1) the benchmark of CRISPR off-target prediction should be properly evaluated and not overestimated by considering data imbalance issue; (2) incorporation of efficient computational techniques (including ensemble learning and data synthesis techniques) can help to address the data imbalance issue and improve the performance of CRISPR off-target prediction. Taking together, we call for more efforts to address the data imbalance issue in CRISPR off-target prediction to facilitate clinical utility of CRISPR-based gene editing techniques.

**Key words:** CRISPR; off-target prediction; data imbalance

# **Introduction**

In the applications of the CRISPR techniques, a major challenge is to accurately predict the genome-wide off-target profile of a given single-guide RNA (sgRNA) and reduce off-targets beforehand [\[1–](#page-6-0)[5\]](#page-6-1). There are several tools proposed for genomewide CRISPR off-target cleavage sites (OTS) prediction and scoring. Among them, the learning-based models were reported to achieve the state-of-the-art performance compared to others [1]. Basically, the learning-based models are trained with known genome-wide off-targets thereby the candidate OTS of a given sgRNA can be scored based on the training model. Recently, several machine learning models have been proposed for CRISPR off-target prediction. For example, the deep learningbased framework, *DeepCRISPR*, has been developed for genomewide CRISPR off-target prediction [5]. Shiran Abadi et al. ## **Calling for proper and objective evaluation of CRISPR off-target prediction performance**

*CRISTA* [6] has made significant contribution to optimized sgRNA design by introducing novel features like the occurrence of bulges [6]. Their study also raises the data imbalance issue in offtarget prediction. As Shiran Abadi et al. [6] noted in their study, the number of samples in the set of uncleaved sites was much higher than the set of cleaved sites and thus combining these two sets into a single training datasets would have resulted in a sharp bias toward the set of uncleaved sites. In CRISTA, Shiran Abadi et al. [6] proposed both leave-one-sgRNA-out and leavestudy-out testing experiments (this can be further divided into the 'common guides' test and the 'unique guides' test). In these experiments, they applied a bootstrapping sampling technique to undersampling the majority class and oversampling the minority in the training datasets resulting in the training dataset with twice the size of the cleaved samples and an equal-sized set of uncleaved samples. Then they evaluated the performances of their model and other models such as CFD [8], CCTop [9] and optCD [10] using the hold-out sgRNA cleaved samples plus an equal-sized set of randomly chosen uncleaved samples from the original testing dataset. Such a testing framework, however, is improperly designed since the prediction was evaluated using an artificial balanced testing dataset instead of the real existing imbalanced dataset, which could overlook the impact of data imbalance that widely exists in genome-wide off-target occurrence. Therefore, we provide the following reanalysis on their model training and evaluation parts and call for proper and objective evaluation of CRISPR off-target prediction performance.

Firstly, the real performance of a machine learning model can only be evaluated by a complete testing dataset regardless of its balancing condition. Based on this point, we keep all the leaveone-sgRNA-out and leave-study-out testing experiments and all datasets [\(Supplementary Figure S1,](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/bib/bbz069#supplementary-data) Supplementary Tables [S1 and S2\) unchanged as mentioned in \[6\] except that all the](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/bib/bbz069#supplementary-data) curated uncleaved samples without undersampling are kept as the hold-out testing datasets. This testing scenario is denoted as 'CRISTA' which is indicated in [Figure 1A–C](#page-2-0) with box plot and [Figure 1D–F](#page-2-0) with bar plot. The bar plots present the averaged performance of different testing models with different testing strategies, and the box plots further illustrate their performance variances. It can be seen that under the measurements of *PR-AUC* and Spearman correlation, the performances of CRISTA decrease substantially compared to their original values reported in [6] [\(Figure 1E and F\)](#page-2-0). For example, the averaged *PR-AUC* drops to 0.52 [\(Figure 1E\)](#page-2-0) compared to their original value of 0.99 in their study for leave-one-sgRNA-out test. It is expected that the averaged *ROC-AUC* performance remains the same with values from previous study [\(Figure 1D\)](#page-2-0) since this measurement is insensitive to the impact of data imbalance. This analysis indicates that their original testing framework is improperly designed although they achieved a higher performance in their study.

Secondly, we would like to point out that the routine training methods without bootstrapping sampling generally perform worse than the methods to the contrary. In this case, we perform the same leave-one-sgRNA-out and leave-study-out testing experiments. The only difference is that all the training samples are fit into the random forest model without bootstrapped sampling. This testing scenario is denoted as 'RF without balanced sampling' as indicated in the bar plot [\(Figure 1D–F\)](#page-2-0) and box plot [\(Figure 1A–C\)](#page-2-0). As expected, 'RF without balanced sampling' performs worse than 'CRISTA' for the leave-sgRNA-out and leavestudy-out: unique guides tests. While for the leave-study-out: common guides test, 'RF without balanced sampling' generally performs better than 'CRISTA'. This is not surprising since during the leave-study-out: common guides testing, the sgRNA guides come from training set and testing set are largely overlapped. Therefore, an over-fitted training procedure without sampling tends to outperform others.

Lastly, based on the random-forest model in [6], we demonstrate that sampling an equal size of positive and negative samples during the training of random forest model (denoted as the 'RF with balanced sampling') could also help to boost the final prediction performance, as shown in [Figure 1A–C](#page-2-0) and [Figure 1D–F.](#page-2-0) Note that this is different from their original study, in which they need to generate a sampling dataset beforehand and perform the whole training-testing procedure for 100 times. This can be considered as a more efficient and solid learning scheme for model building. In this test scenario, there are no requirements for the training dataset containing equal size of positive and negative data beforehand. In other words, the training data can be stratified with identical proportion of positive and negative samples as those in the background population, which mimics the real CRISPR off-target prediction scenario. Since the random forest model is intrinsically a bootstrapping sampling-based ensemble learning model, we can seamlessly perform the bootstrapping sampling with the constraint of equal size of subsets of positive and negative sampling during the training procedure. The advantage of this computational model is that it helps to save 100 times of computation compared to the original testing framework [6]. As shown in [Figure 1A–C,](#page-2-0) 'RF with balanced sampling' achieves generally equal or better performances under different measurements compared to 'CRISTA'.

## **Calling for efficient computational techniques to address the data imbalance issue and improve the performance of CRISPR off-target prediction**

*Elevation* is another well-established learning model for genomewide OTS prediction [7]. In their study, the two-layered model was established. Particularly, the published GUIDE-seq data comprising nine sgRNAs assessed for off-target cleavage activity were used to train the second layer. These sgRNAs yielded 354 active off-target sites with up to six mismatches. Inactive sites were obtained by *Cas-OFFinder* [11] to identify all 294 534 sites with six or fewer mismatches as the negative sample pool. *Elevation* selected 355 inactive samples among all negative samples with customized interval to conduct their training process. Metrics of weighted spearman correlation and ROC-AUC were used as the benchmark of Elevation with other OTS prediction models, such as CFD [8], HsuZhang [12] and CCTop [9] on two independent test datasets from original GUIDE-seq datasets. Weighted Spearman correlation is the Spearman correlation between values predicted by the learning model and the measured values of testing data weighted by a standard error or other indexes. Although the weighted Spearman correlation emphasizes the minority samples in a data imbalance scenario, it is suitable for regression rather than classification and it is not necessarily intuitive to understand as reported in the work of Listgarten et al. [7]. In our study, we prefer to use PR-AUC instead of weighted Spearman correlation since here, we generally formulate the OTS prediction as a classification problem.

Firstly, we point out that the training model with 354 active samples and 355 inactive samples of *Elevation* can be boosted with the bootstrapping sampling-based ensemble learning techniques as adopted previously in *CRISTA* [6], *DeepCRISPR* [5] and others [13]. In our study, bootstrapping sampling technique is applied to sample the 355 negative samples from the negative samples pool for 831 times. The individual training model is trained the same as *Elevation* and 831 trained models are combined together in an ensemble learning way for the final prediction. The prediction performance is tested on the two independent GUIDE-seq datasets as mentioned in *Elevation*. The first dataset is acquired from the wild-type Cas9 experiments [8] (five unique sgRNAs) and the other is obtained from the newly generated experiments by Listgarten et al. [7] (22 unique sgRNAs). We use *Cas-OFFinder* to identify sites of 5 sgRNAs and 12 sgRNAs with off-target sites detected from the original 22 sgRNAs with six or fewer mismatches as potential off-targets to obtain the two independent test datasets from the original GUIDE-seq datasets. Test dataset 1 consists of five sgRNAs yielding a total of 312 113 potential off-targets, of which 58 are active [\(Supplementary Table S3\)](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/bib/bbz069#supplementary-data), and Test dataset 2 consists of 12 unique sgRNAs yielding a total of 749 169 potential off-targets, among which 68 are active [\(Supplementary Table S4\)](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/bib/bbz069#supplementary-data). Data imbalance distribution of the whole datasets used in our work is ref lected in [Supplementary Figure S1](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/bib/bbz069#supplementary-data) and Supplementary Table S5. *ROC-AUC* and *PR-AUC* are reported in [Figure 2A–D](#page-3-0) for two testing data, respectively. As shown in [Figure 2A–D,](#page-3-0) ensemble learning technique helps to improve the off-target prediction performance both evaluated in *ROC-AUC* and *PR-AUC*. Compared with the original version of *Elevation*, improvements of 3.11% and 2.88% in *ROC-AUC* on the two independent testing datasets are achieved, respectively. Particularly, ensemble learning technique helps to improve the *PR-AUC* substantially, with the improvements of 14.74% and 11.59% on the two independent testing datasets, respectively. Improvements of ROC-AUC indicate that our proposed strategies can achieve the high accuracy in the distinguishing of cleaved sites or uncleaved sites in OTS prediction. Secondly, we point out that the original *Elevation* model can also be boosted by another computational technique, i.e the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) to address the data imbalance issue. Basically, SMOTE [14] is an oversampling approach in which the minority class is oversampled by creating 'synthetic' examples rather than by oversampling with replacements. The specific process of SMOTE is that the minority class is oversampled by taking each minority class sample and introducing synthetic examples along the line segments joining any/all of the *k* minority class nearest neighbors. In our study, SMOTE is applied on the original 354 positive training data to generate the 'synthetic' positive samples with the same number of the negative samples. For this case, the same training model is applied as that of *Elevation* while training on the syntheticgenerated training data. The prediction performance is also tested on the two independent testing datasets and the *ROC-AUC* and *PR-AUC* are reported in [Figure 2E–H](#page-3-0) for two testing data, respectively. As shown in [Figure 2E–H,](#page-3-0) SMOTE technique also helps to improve the off-target prediction performance both evaluated in *ROC-AUC* and *PR-AUC*. Comparing to the original version of *Elevation*, improvements of 3.11% and 2.88% in *ROC-AUC* on the two independent testing datasets are achieved, respectively. Similarly, SMOTE technique also helps to improve the *PR-AUC* substantially, with the improvements of 11.05% and 10.68% on the two independent testing datasets, respectively.

Lastly, to further benchmark the performance of our approach with other tools, we collect two independent datasets from two cell lines: testing dataset 3 with 18 sgRNAs from HEK293 cell line [\[12,](#page-6-8) [15](#page-6-11)[–19\]](#page-6-12) and testing dataset 4 with 12 sgRNAs from K562 cell line [20]. Testing dataset 3 contains 132 914 sgRNA pairs, among which 536 of them are active. Testing dataset 4 contains 20 319 sgRNA pairs, among which 120 of them are active. The *PR-AUC* and *ROC-AUC* of testing dataset 3 and testing dataset 4 are calculated for ensemble technique and data synthetic technique, respectively [\(Figure 3\)](#page-4-0). It is clearly shown that after using the ensemble technique and data synthetic technique, the performance of our proposed strategies are all improved especially for *PR-AUC* in the two datasets compared with their original Elevation score model. Our techniques achieve a better performance using these techniques due to models trained in a more balanced way. In addition, these computational techniques not only improve the performance of OTS prediction model, but also save computation time since there is no need to generate artificial balanced testing datasets for 100 times as applied in Elevation [7].

## **Conclusion**

In summary, taking two well-established learning model as examples, our study indicates that (1) the benchmark of CRISPR off-target prediction should be properly evaluated and not overestimated by considering data imbalance issue; (2) data imbalance issue can be addressed to certain extent with the help of carefully designed computational techniques. Incorporating bootstrapping sampling technique or data synthetic technique can help to boost the CRISPR off-target prediction performance as reported in our study. Due to the data imbalance issue, it is very challenging to accurately predict the genome-wide CRISPR off-target profile. Nevertheless, we have witnessed continuous progresses made in this area [\[1,](#page-6-0) [5–](#page-6-1)[7,](#page-6-3) [13\]](#page-6-9). Most of the current tools, including *CRISTA*, *Elevation* and *DeepCRISPR*, tend to avoid missing true OTS by weighing higher on positive samples. Although this makes sense for CRISPR-based gene therapy since the penalty of missing a true off-target site is always higher than that of inducing a false positive in off-target site prediction, future improvement with reduced false positives are still encouraged. In addition, different models should be designed carefully and tested in the large-scale unbiased datasets to investigate their performance. Beside our proposed techniques that follow the concept of cost sensitive learning [21], other computational techniques can be further investigated to achieve a better CRISPR off-target profile prediction.

#### **Key Points**

- We emphasize the data imbalance issue in CRISPR offtarget prediction, i.e. the number of true OTS recognized by whole-genome off-target detection techniques is much smaller than that of all possible nucleotide mismatch loci.
- We present a comprehensive analysis to indicate that the benchmark of CRISPR off-target prediction should be properly evaluated and not overestimated by considering data imbalance issue.
- We present a comprehensive analysis to indicate that incorporation of efficient computational techniques (including ensemble learning and data synthesis techniques) can help to address the data imbalance issue and improve the performance of CRISPR off-target prediction.
